# Blood Tests Guideline Reference

Hemoglobin (Hb) is a protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to the rest of the body. Normal levels typically range from 13.5 to 17.5 g/dL in men and 12.0 to 15.5 g/dL in women. Low levels indicate anemia, leading to fatigue and weakness, while high levels might suggest polycythemia or dehydration.

White Blood Cells (WBC) are a crucial part of the immune system. Normal counts range from 4.5 to 11.0 × 10^9/L. An elevated WBC count (leukocytosis) often indicates an infection, inflammation, or immune system disorder. A low count (leukopenia) makes a person more susceptible to infections and can be caused by viral illnesses, bone marrow disorders, or certain medications.

Fasting blood glucose measures the amount of sugar in the blood after an overnight fast. Normal values are between 70 and 99 mg/dL. Levels between 100 and 125 mg/dL suggest prediabetes, while levels of 126 mg/dL or higher indicate diabetes. High glucose levels (hyperglycemia) can damage blood vessels, while low levels (hypoglycemia) can cause dizziness and confusion.

A lipid panel measures total cholesterol, HDL (good), LDL (bad) cholesterol, and triglycerides. Total cholesterol should ideally be below 200 mg/dL. High LDL levels increase the risk of plaque buildup in arteries, leading to heart disease. High HDL helps clear cholesterol from the blood, offering protective cardiovascular effects.

Creatinine is a waste product from muscle breakdown, normally filtered by the kidneys. Typical levels are 0.74 to 1.35 mg/dL for men and 0.59 to 1.04 mg/dL for women. Elevated creatinine is a strong indicator of impaired kidney function, whereas lower levels are generally not a cause for concern.

Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) controls the production of thyroid hormones. Normal values are around 0.4 to 4.0 mIU/L. High TSH indicates hypothyroidism (an underactive thyroid), causing fatigue and weight gain. Low TSH suggests hyperthyroidism (an overactive thyroid), leading to weight loss, rapid heart rate, and anxiety.

Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) are enzymes found primarily in the liver. Normal levels are typically below 40 U/L. Elevated levels of these enzymes in the blood suggest liver damage or inflammation, which can be caused by fatty liver disease, hepatitis, alcohol consumption, or certain medications.

Vitamin D is essential for bone health and immune function. Normal levels are generally considered 20 ng/mL or above, though optimal levels might be higher. Deficiency can lead to weakened bones, fatigue, and muscle pain. It is commonly synthesized in the skin via sunlight or obtained through diet and supplements.

Platelets (thrombocytes) help the blood clot to stop bleeding. Normal counts are 150,000 to 450,000 per microliter. Low platelets (thrombocytopenia) can cause easy bruising or bleeding, while high counts (thrombocytosis) can increase the risk of inappropriate blood clots.

Potassium is an electrolyte critical for nerve and muscle function, especially the heart. Normal levels are 3.6 to 5.2 mmol/L. High potassium (hyperkalemia) or low potassium (hypokalemia) can both cause dangerous heart arrhythmias and muscle weakness.

Sodium is an electrolyte that regulates fluid balance and nerve function. Normal levels are 135 to 145 mEq/L. Low sodium (hyponatremia) can cause confusion, nausea, and seizures. High sodium (hypernatremia) is usually related to dehydration and can cause thirst and central nervous system dysfunction.

Calcium is vital for bone strength, nerve signaling, and muscle contraction. Normal total blood calcium is about 8.5 to 10.2 mg/dL. Abnormal levels can indicate parathyroid gland issues, kidney disease, or Vitamin D imbalances, affecting bone density and nerve activity.

Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN) measures the amount of urea nitrogen in the blood, a waste product from protein breakdown. Normal ranges are 7 to 20 mg/dL. Elevated BUN can point to kidney dysfunction, dehydration, or a high-protein diet.
